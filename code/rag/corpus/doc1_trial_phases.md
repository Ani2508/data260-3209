# Clinical Trial Phases

Clinical trials that test a new drug or treatment in humans are organized into
sequential phases. Each phase answers a different question and builds on the
results of the phase before it.

## Phase I

Phase I trials are the first time a new treatment is given to humans. These
trials are small, usually enrolling 20 to 100 healthy volunteers or patients.
The main goal is to test safety: researchers look for side effects, determine
a safe dosage range, and see how the drug is absorbed, metabolized, and
eliminated by the body. Phase I trials do not usually test whether the
treatment works, only whether it is safe enough to continue testing.

## Phase II

Phase II trials enroll a larger group, typically 100 to 300 participants who
have the condition the treatment is meant to address. The main goal shifts to
effectiveness: does the treatment actually help? Researchers continue to
monitor safety, but now also compare outcomes, sometimes against a placebo or
an existing treatment. Many treatments fail during Phase II because they turn
out not to work well enough, even if they were safe in Phase I.

## Phase III

Phase III trials are large, often enrolling several hundred to several
thousand participants across multiple hospitals or sites. These trials
directly compare the new treatment to the current standard treatment (or a
placebo, when no standard exists). Phase III is the last major step before a
regulatory agency, such as the FDA, decides whether to approve the treatment
for general use. Because of their size, Phase III trials take the longest and
cost the most to run.

## Phase IV

Phase IV trials happen after a treatment has already been approved and is
being sold to the public. These are sometimes called post-marketing studies.
The goal is to monitor long-term safety and effectiveness in a much larger and
more diverse population than could be studied before approval. Phase IV
trials can uncover rare side effects that did not appear in the smaller,
earlier phases.

## Why phases matter

Moving through phases in order protects participants: unsafe or ineffective
treatments are meant to be filtered out early, before large numbers of people
are exposed to them. A trial's phase is usually listed clearly in its public
registration record, alongside its recruitment status.
