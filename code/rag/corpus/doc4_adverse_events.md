# Adverse Event Reporting in Clinical Trials

An adverse event is any unwanted or unexpected medical problem that happens to
a participant during a clinical trial, whether or not it is believed to be
caused by the treatment being studied. Tracking and reporting these events
carefully is one of the most important safety responsibilities in running a
trial.

## Types of adverse events

Adverse events are generally classified by severity:

- Mild: causes minor discomfort but does not interfere with daily activities.
- Moderate: causes enough discomfort to interfere with daily activities, but
  is not dangerous.
- Severe: significantly interferes with daily activities or requires medical
  intervention.
- Serious Adverse Event (SAE): results in death, is life-threatening,
  requires hospitalization, causes lasting disability, or causes a birth
  defect. SAEs must be reported on a much faster timeline than other events.

## Who must report

The trial's investigators (the doctors and researchers running the study at
each site) are responsible for recording every adverse event a participant
experiences, regardless of whether it seems related to the treatment. This
information is then reported to the trial's sponsor and, for serious events,
often to a regulatory agency and an independent oversight board.

## Reporting timelines

Serious adverse events typically must be reported within a short, strict
window -- often 24 to 72 hours -- after the study team becomes aware of them.
Non-serious adverse events are usually collected and reported on a routine
schedule, such as at each participant visit, rather than immediately.

## Data and Safety Monitoring Boards

Larger trials, especially Phase III trials, often have an independent Data
and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB). This board periodically reviews the
adverse event data without being involved in running the trial day to day. If
the DSMB sees a pattern of unacceptable harm, or already sees a clear benefit
that makes continuing the trial unethical, it can recommend the trial be
modified or terminated early.

## Why this matters

Careful adverse event reporting protects current participants by allowing
early warning signs to be caught quickly, and it protects future patients by
making sure the treatment's real safety profile is accurately understood
before it might be approved for wider use.
