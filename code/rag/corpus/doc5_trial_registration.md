# Clinical Trial Registration Requirements

Before a clinical trial can enroll participants, it generally must be
registered in a public clinical trial registry. This requirement increases
transparency and helps prevent selective reporting of only favorable results.

## NCT numbers

In the United States, trials are registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, a
database run by the National Library of Medicine. Once registered, a trial is
assigned a unique identifier called an NCT number (for example, NCT01234567).
This number is used to reference the trial in publications, consent forms,
and other trials that may build on its results.

## What must be registered

A complete trial registration typically includes:

- The official title of the study and a brief summary of its purpose.
- The condition or disease being studied.
- The intervention being tested (a drug, device, procedure, or behavioral
  treatment).
- Eligibility criteria, describing who can and cannot join.
- The sponsor or institution responsible for the trial.
- The current recruitment status of the trial.
- Contact information for the study team.
- The trial's phase, if it involves testing a drug or biologic.

## When registration must happen

Many journals and regulatory agencies require that a trial be registered
before it enrolls its first participant. Registering late, or only after
results are known, undermines the purpose of registration, since it becomes
easier to selectively register only trials with favorable outcomes.

## Updating the registration

A trial's registry entry is not static. Sponsors are expected to keep it
updated as the study progresses, especially the recruitment status field
(such as changing it from Recruiting to Active, Not Recruiting, and
eventually to Completed or Terminated). Results are also expected to be
submitted to the registry within a set period after the trial ends, though in
practice reporting delays are common and are considered a compliance problem
that certain agencies actively track.

## Why public registries matter

A public, searchable registry lets patients find trials they may be eligible
to join, lets researchers avoid unnecessarily duplicating studies that are
already underway, and lets the public and other scientists hold sponsors
accountable for reporting results, including unfavorable ones.
